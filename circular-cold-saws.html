Cold saws — circular cold saws, manual to CNC carbide

A cold saw cuts metal with a slow-turning, coolant-flooded circular blade instead of a hot abrasive disc, leaving a square, burr-free end that is cool to the touch and ready to weld. Three families of cold saw machines: ISITAN's wet-type circular cold saws for the fabrication shop — the SFC 275 and 315 pull-down saws with a manual or pneumatic vise and the semi-automatic SFC 350-VX with a hydro-pneumatic head; CSM's CT325 automatic saw with hydraulic down-feed and a servo carriage, cutting tube and bundles to Ø4″ in three spindle-speed ranges; and Bekamak's automatic production saws: the BMDO XS high-speed carbide (TCT) cold saws that cut solid bar to Ø6-1/4″ from a 20 ft magazine loader with servo feeding, the OPUS 300 with a Ø35-7/16″ (900 mm) carbide blade and a 50 HP spindle for solid bar to Ø11-3/4″, and the BMDO CS HSS saws for tube and bar to Ø4″ with a servo gripper feed and touch-screen control. Mitre cuts 45° both ways on the ISITAN saws, blades for mild, high-strength and stainless steel, CSA-approved panels and support from Mississauga, ON, Canada. NovoTech's value-line cold saws, built in China, add gear-driven manual saws with 9 – 14″ blades, a semi-automatic hydraulic saw, a 16″ heavy-duty saw and abrasive cut-off saws, plus roller stands, a roller conveyor with a digital length stop and a measuring roller table.

  • Manual and semi-automatic circular cold saws: round to Ø4-1/2″, mitre 45° both ways, 10-3/4″ – 13-3/4″ HSS blades
  • Automatic carbide cold saws: solid bar Ø3/8″ – 6-1/4″ at up to 920 ft/min, servo feed, 20 ft magazine loader — and the OPUS 300 for bar to Ø11-3/4″
  • Automatic HSS cold saws: tube, bundles and bar to Ø4″, servo feed to ±.004″, several lengths per bar — CSM CT325 and Bekamak BMDO CS
  • NovoTech value line: CS cold saws 9 – 14″, PDS-315H semi-automatic hydraulic, PDS-400 heavy-duty 16″, TV abrasive saws 12 – 16″

Cold saw, abrasive saw or band saw?

All three cut bar, tube and profiles to length, but they leave very different ends.

  • Cold saw. A toothed circular blade turns slowly under coolant and leaves a square, burr-free end that is cool to the touch and ready to weld or fit. It cuts tube and profiles in seconds, and the blade is resharpened many times.
  • Abrasive saw. A cut-off wheel grinds through the steel at high speed. The saw costs the least, but the end is hot, burred and slightly bevelled, sparks fly, and the wheels are thrown away as they wear down.
  • Band saw. A band saw cuts large solids, wide sections and bundles that no cold saw can take, with a low blade cost per cut. On small tube and profiles it is slower per cut than a cold saw.

The usual choice in a fabrication shop: a cold saw for the many cuts in tube and profiles up to about 4″ where the finish and the angle matter, and a band saw for big material.

HSS or carbide blades?

HSS blades run slowly, typically 45 – 90 rpm on a 10 – 14″ blade, with flood coolant. They are the standard blade for tube, thin walls, profiles and mixed work, and they are resharpened dozens of times. Coated blades last longer in harder material: TiN for medium-hard steel, TiCN for high-strength steel and TiAlN for stainless.

Carbide (TCT) blades run several times faster and are the fastest way to cut solid steel bar in production, but they need a saw built for them: high spindle speed, a rigid head, servo feeding and steady clamping. They also cost more per blade and per resharpening, so they pay off in long runs.

Choose the tooth pitch so that at least two or three teeth are in the wall of a thin tube, and a coarser pitch for solid bar, so each tooth gap can hold its chip. On the ISITAN SFC saws the blade bore is 32 mm with two Ø11 mm pin holes, a standard that is easy to source in Canada.

Capacity and miter cuts

The blade diameter sets the capacity. Every saw has a chart of the largest round tube, square tube, rectangular profile and solid bar it cuts at 90° and at each miter angle. Capacity drops as the head swivels, so check your largest profile at the angle you cut it, not only at 90°. Most fabrication saws miter both ways, to 45° left and right or more. A vise that clamps close to the blade on both sides gives a clean, square cut, and a bundle vise cuts several tubes at once. Solid bar takes far longer to cut than tube of the same diameter, so if most of your cutting is solid bar above about 2″, a band saw or a carbide saw is the better machine.

Manual, semi-automatic or automatic?

  • Manual pull-down saw. The operator clamps the vise and pulls the head down by hand. It is the simplest and lowest-priced saw, for occasional cuts and short runs.
  • Pneumatic vise. The vise clamps with air at the pull of a lever, which saves time on every cut and holds short pieces better.
  • Semi-automatic saw. The head descends at a regulated rate from a foot switch or start button, then returns. It is for batches of the same part with consistent feed and blade life.
  • Automatic saw. A servo gripper or carriage feeds the bar, and the saw clamps, cuts and counts to a list of lengths and quantities, typically to about ±.004″. Some saws load bars from a magazine and run unattended.

Add roller stands or a roller table with a length stop on the infeed and outfeed side for long bars. They make even a manual saw much faster and more accurate.

Speed, coolant and installation

Two blade speeds, or an inverter with variable speed, let one saw cut steel at low speed and aluminum and copper faster. Stainless needs the low speed, a sharp coated blade and a steady feed: let the blade rub without cutting and stainless work-hardens. Keep the coolant at the concentration the maker gives. It cools the blade, flushes the chips and is the biggest single factor in blade life. Cold saws need little more than a solid floor and power: 600 V three-phase with a CSA-approved panel as standard (208, 240 or 480 V on request), and 240 V single-phase on the smallest models. Tell us your largest tube, profile and solid bar, the angles you cut and your batch sizes, and we will recommend the saw and the blade.

Questions we get asked about cold saws

Cold saw or abrasive chop saw?

An abrasive chop saw grinds through the steel at high speed, leaving a hot, burred, slightly bevelled end and sparks. A cold saw cuts with a slow HSS blade (45–90 rpm) under coolant, so the cut is square, cool to the touch, burr-free and ready to weld or fit — and the blade is resharpened dozens of times instead of thrown away.

TCT carbide or HSS cold saw?

A carbide (TCT) blade runs at 130–920 ft/min and is the fastest way to cut solid steel bar in production — the Bekamak BMDO XS saws and the Ø11-3/4″ OPUS 300 are built around it, with servo feeding and a magazine loader for unattended runs. HSS blades run slower (22–130 ft/min on the BMDO CS) and are the choice for tube, thin walls and mixed work; the XS saws accept HSS blades for tube as well.

What does an automatic cold saw do by itself?

You load bars in the magazine (BMDO XS) or on the roller table (BMDO CS), program the lengths and quantities, and the saw feeds each bar with a servo gripper, clamps it hydraulically, cuts, counts and moves on to the next length — to ±.004″ on the CS and with a 60 mm remnant on the XS.

What is the CSM CT325 for?

Cutting tube to length in production, single or in bundles: the CT325 clamps hydraulically, feeds with a servo carriage to ±.004″ at 39 in/s and feeds the blade down hydraulically through planetary gears, with a PLC touch-screen and a pedestal with emergency stop. The L, M and H types differ only in spindle speed — steel, stainless and alloy, aluminum and non-ferrous. It is the saw CSM builds into its bending cells, and stands just as well on its own.

Manual, pneumatic or semi-automatic?

The SFC V clamps with a hand vise — the simplest saw for occasional cuts. The SFC VP clamps with air at the pull of a lever, which saves time on every cut and holds short pieces better. The SFC 350-VX runs a semi-automatic cycle from a foot switch with a regulated head descent, for batches of the same part without an operator on the handle.

Which blade do I need?

A standard HSS blade cuts mild steel and aluminum. For medium-hard steel we fit TiN-coated blades, TiCN for high-strength steel and TiAlN for stainless. The blade bore is 32 mm with two Ø11 mm pin holes on all SFC models, so blades are readily available in Canada.

Cold saw or abrasive cut-off saw?

A cold saw cuts with a toothed HSS or carbide blade at low speed: a square, burr-free cut that is ready to weld, no sparks and no heat in the part, but the blade must suit the material. An abrasive saw grinds through with a 12 – 16″ wheel at high speed: a lower price, any steel including hardened, but a hot part, sparks, a burr and wheels that wear quickly. For production cutting of tube and profiles choose a cold saw; for occasional cutting of mixed steel the abrasive saw.

What voltage do these machines run on?

Three-phase machines are supplied for 600 V as standard with a CSA-approved panel; 208 V, 240 V and 480 V 3-phase are available on request, and 240 V single-phase on models whose main motor is 2.2 kW (3 HP) or less — a machine with a motor above 2.2 kW must run on three-phase power and cannot be converted to 240 V single-phase. Manual machines have no electrics.
