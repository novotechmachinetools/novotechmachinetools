Pipe benders and tube bending machines — CNC, NC, all-electric and hydraulic, Ø15/32″ to Ø8-5/8″

A pipe bender or tube bending machine draws a tube around a bend die to a set angle; a mandrel bender adds an internal mandrel so thin-wall tube bends to a tight radius without wrinkling or collapsing. NovoTech sells two lines of mandrel benders in Canada, each on its own pages: ISITAN, built in Turkey since 1959 — servo CNC mandrel benders with 3D simulation, NC hydraulic mandrel benders with a 10″ touchscreen, compact SFB 32 and aluminum-profile benders, and BK rotary draw benders for pipe, bar and box section, with the premium-line SPB NC draw benders and TB 34 and TB 42 mandrel-less pipe benders beside them; and CSM, built in Taiwan since 1982 — all-electric EMB / EMR benders that bend left- and right-hand in one part, MBE / MRE and T-series electric hybrids with stacked tools, TSR / TDR hydraulic CNC and TNCB NC pipe benders to Ø8-5/8″ and two-head symmetric benders. From a 1 HP bench bender for 1-1/4″ pipe to a 20 ft hydraulic CNC pipe bender for shipyard pipe, every machine is supplied with a CSA-approved panel, tooling and installation, and supported from Mississauga, ON, Canada. Below, each page in brief and every model in one capacity table.

  • CSM tube benders — all-electric, hybrid and hydraulic CNC, NC and symmetric benders, Ø15/32″ to Ø8-5/8″

Tube or pipe — and the numbers that describe a bend

Pipe is sized by nominal pipe size (NPS) and schedule, tube by its outside diameter (OD) and wall. The two are not the same: 1-1/2″ schedule 40 pipe is 1.900″ OD, while 1-1/2″ tube is 1.500″ OD. The bend die has to fit the actual OD exactly. Four numbers describe every bend, and together they decide the machine and the tooling:

  • CLR — the centreline radius of the bend.
  • D of bend — the CLR divided by the OD. A 3″ CLR on 1-1/2″ tube is a 2D bend.
  • Wall factor — the OD divided by the wall. A 1-1/2 × 0.065″ tube has a wall factor of 23. The higher it is, the thinner the wall for its size.
  • Degree of bend — the angle, usually up to 180°.

Tight bends (low D) in thin walls (high wall factor) are the hard ones.

Which bending method?

  • Push-type (compression) benders. They push the pipe into a die between two supports. They are simple, quick and inexpensive for plumbing, electrical and maintenance pipe, where some flattening is acceptable.
  • Rotary draw benders. They clamp the tube to a turning bend die and draw it around, supported by a pressure die. The bend is tight and accurate with little flattening, which is the method for handrails, roll cages, frames, furniture and exhaust. Without a mandrel, they suit thicker walls and radii of about 2D and up.
  • Mandrel benders. They are rotary draw benders with a mandrel inside the tube, and often a wiper die behind the bend. Thin-wall tube then bends to tight radii without wrinkling on the inside or collapsing on the outside.
  • Roll bending. Three rolls form large radii, rings and spirals. That is the job of the tube rollers and angle rolls. As a rule of thumb, a die bender is the tool up to a radius of about 5 times the tube diameter; above that, and for any arc longer than one die would cover, a roll bender is cheaper and quicker, because one set of rolls makes every radius.

When do I need a mandrel?

Without support from inside, a tube bent tight wrinkles on the inside of the bend and flattens on the outside. As a guide in mild steel, plan on a mandrel when the CLR is under about twice the OD, or when the wall factor rises above about 20 – 25. Tighter radii and thinner walls add a wiper die, then a ball mandrel with more balls. Stainless, aluminum and thin exhaust tube need support sooner than thick mild-steel tube. Every ISITAN SFB bender has a hydraulic mandrel and bends with or without it, so one machine covers both kinds of work. Send us the tube, the wall and the CLR, and we will tell you what tooling the bend needs.

Manual, NC or CNC?

Every bend in a multi-bend part has three values: the distance to the next bend (feed), the rotation of the tube between bends (the plane of bend) and the angle. Machines differ in how many of these they control:

  • Manual and hydraulic benders. The operator sets all three, with stops and a scale. For occasional bends and repair work.
  • NC benders. The control stores and bends the angles, and the operator feeds and rotates the tube to stops. A carrier with servo rotation, as on the SFB NC/C, adds repeatable rotation. For mixed work, exhaust, handrails and furniture, an NC bender pays back fastest.
  • CNC benders. They drive feed, rotation and angle with servo motors from a program or a drawing, with springback compensation, so a part with ten bends in different planes comes off the same every time. For series production.

The drive is the other choice. Hydraulic benders give the force for large pipe. All-electric servo benders are faster, quieter and more precise, and switch parts quickly, which suits small and medium tube in series work.

Tooling, springback and what to send us

Each tube size and each CLR needs its own tooling: the bend die, the clamp die, the pressure die, and for mandrel bending the mandrel and the wiper die. Every bender comes with one set in the size you ask for, and CNC benders can carry stacked tools for several radii or sizes in one part. Springback, the angle the tube opens up after the bend, grows with the strength of the material and the radius. The NC or CNC compensates for it once it has been measured on the first part. Questions we get asked about pipe benders

Pipe bender, tube bender, mandrel bender — what is the difference?

"Pipe" is sized by nominal pipe size (NPS) and schedule, "tube" by outside diameter and wall, but the machines are the same: a bend die, a clamp and a pressure die draw the material around the radius. A mandrel bender adds an internal mandrel so thin-wall tube can be bent to a tight radius without wrinkling; our SFB benders are mandrel benders, the BK series are rotary draw benders without a mandrel. Every capacity on these pages is given as OD × wall in inches and gauge.

How do I choose a tube bender?

Start with the largest tube (OD × wall and material), the tightest centreline radius and the number of bends per part — that sets mandrel or not and the model size. Then the volume: a rotary draw BK for occasional bends, an SFB NC for programmed angles with manual feed, the NC/C carrier for repeatable multi-plane parts, and a CNC when parts come from a drawing in hundreds.
