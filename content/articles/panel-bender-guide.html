How does a panel bender work?

The blank lies flat on a table of brushes or balls. A manipulator pushes it to the bending line and the blank-holder (the upper tool) clamps it. A C-shaped blade carrier then moves around the clamped edge and folds the flange: a positive bend when the blade moves up, a negative bend when it moves down. The manipulator turns the blank and presents the next side, and so on until all four sides are done. Because the blades and the blank-holder are driven by servo axes, the same tools make 90° bends and any other angle, hems, offsets, closed profiles and large radii, which the machine forms as a series of small bends.

The difference from a press brake is that the operator never lifts, flips or supports the part, and nothing has to be changed between parts. On machines with automatic tool adjustment, the blank-holder sets its own length for each side.

When does a panel bender pay off?

A panel bender earns its price on panels with flanges on two, three or four sides, in thin and medium sheet:

  • cabinets, enclosures and electrical boxes
  • door skins, shelving and store fixtures
  • HVAC casings and air-handling panels
  • appliance and elevator panels
  • lighting housings

Three things make the saving:

  • Batch size stops mattering. There is no tool change, so a kit of one of each part runs as fast as a batch of a hundred. It is the natural machine to put behind a laser or a punch.
  • Labour. One operator loads the blank and takes the part away, or nobody does if a robot loads it. A large panel on a press brake needs a skilled operator and often a helper.
  • Consistency. The first part is the same as the thousandth, without trial bends and without the angle changing with the operator.

It is not the machine for plate, for parts with one long bend, for deep narrow channels or for very small parts. Those stay on the press brake, and most shops that buy a panel bender keep their brake.

How is the capacity of a panel bender rated?

Compare machines on five numbers:

  • Maximum thickness. It is given in mild steel. Stainless is roughly three-quarters of that and aluminum about one and a half times: for example 14 ga (2.0 mm) mild steel, 16 ga (1.5 mm) stainless and 1/8″ (3.0 mm) aluminum.
  • Bending length. From about 47″ to 165″. Choose it for the longest side you bend, not the longest blank.
  • Maximum flange height. Typically 6 – 7″; taller on request.
  • Smallest part. The minimum blank and the minimum inside size of a four-side box, for example 5.5 × 7.5″.
  • Largest blank the manipulator can turn.

The quickest way to choose is to send us three parts: your largest panel, your smallest box and the part with the tallest flange. If those three bend on a machine, the rest of the work almost always will.

How do the panel benders differ?

All the machines on this page bend at about 0.2 s per bend with servo-driven blades. They differ in how they hold and turn the blank, and in how the tooling is set for a new part.

  • Dener panel bender. A servo manipulator on a brush table, with a pressing arm and 15 servo axes, turns blanks up to 78.7 × 49.2″.
  • NovoTech PB-M. A press arm feeds and supports the blank, and a suction-cup manipulator turns it. It comes in nine widths from 63″ to 165″.
  • NovoTech PB-A. The same machine with an automatic tool changer, which re-sets the blank-holder tools in about 0.5 s for every side and notch of the part.
  • Senfeng BDC. Push-down machines from 59″ to 126″ with 13 to 16 servo axes, plus light suction-cup models for thin door and appliance panels.

The tooling choice matters most for a high-mix shop. With manual adjustment, the operator sets the length of the blank-holder for each new part. With automatic tool change (ATC), the machine sets it for every side by itself. If you bend many different parts a day, ATC is the option that keeps the machine bending instead of being set up.

Programming, automation and what the machine needs on site

Parts are programmed from the 3D model or a DXF. The software unfolds the part, chooses the bending sequence, checks for collisions and compensates for springback, so a new part runs in minutes without test bends, and programs can be prepared offline. Loading and unloading options range from a simple stack loader to two robots that feed blanks from a pallet and stack the finished parts. With them, the panel bender can run unattended behind a laser.

All-electric panel benders draw little power (about 2.8 kW on average for the Dener machine). They need a level floor and, on some models, compressed air.
