Tube or pipe — and the numbers that describe a bend

Pipe is sized by nominal pipe size (NPS) and schedule, tube by its outside diameter (OD) and wall. The two are not the same: 1-1/2″ schedule 40 pipe is 1.900″ OD, while 1-1/2″ tube is 1.500″ OD. The bend die has to fit the actual OD exactly. Four numbers describe every bend, and together they decide the machine and the tooling:

  • CLR — the centreline radius of the bend.
  • D of bend — the CLR divided by the OD. A 3″ CLR on 1-1/2″ tube is a 2D bend.
  • Wall factor — the OD divided by the wall. A 1-1/2 × 0.065″ tube has a wall factor of 23. The higher it is, the thinner the wall for its size.
  • Degree of bend — the angle, usually up to 180°.

Tight bends (low D) in thin walls (high wall factor) are the hard ones.

Which bending method?

  • Push-type (compression) benders. They push the pipe into a die between two supports. They are simple, quick and inexpensive for plumbing, electrical and maintenance pipe, where some flattening is acceptable.
  • Rotary draw benders. They clamp the tube to a turning bend die and draw it around, supported by a pressure die. The bend is tight and accurate with little flattening, which is the method for handrails, roll cages, frames, furniture and exhaust. Without a mandrel, they suit thicker walls and radii of about 2D and up.
  • Mandrel benders. They are rotary draw benders with a mandrel inside the tube, and often a wiper die behind the bend. Thin-wall tube then bends to tight radii without wrinkling on the inside or collapsing on the outside.
  • Roll bending. Three rolls form large radii, rings and spirals. That is the job of the tube rollers and angle rolls. As a rule of thumb, a die bender is the tool up to a radius of about 5 times the tube diameter; above that, and for any arc longer than one die would cover, a roll bender is cheaper and quicker, because one set of rolls makes every radius.

When do I need a mandrel?

Without support from inside, a tube bent tight wrinkles on the inside of the bend and flattens on the outside. As a guide in mild steel, plan on a mandrel when the CLR is under about twice the OD, or when the wall factor rises above about 20 – 25. Tighter radii and thinner walls add a wiper die, then a ball mandrel with more balls. Stainless, aluminum and thin exhaust tube need support sooner than thick mild-steel tube. Manual, NC or CNC?

Every bend in a multi-bend part has three values: the distance to the next bend (feed), the rotation of the tube between bends (the plane of bend) and the angle. Machines differ in how many of these they control:

  • Manual and hydraulic benders. The operator sets all three, with stops and a scale. For occasional bends and repair work.
  • NC benders. The control stores and bends the angles, and the operator feeds and rotates the tube to stops. A carrier with servo rotation, as on the SFB NC/C, adds repeatable rotation. For mixed work, exhaust, handrails and furniture, an NC bender pays back fastest.
  • CNC benders. They drive feed, rotation and angle with servo motors from a program or a drawing, with springback compensation, so a part with ten bends in different planes comes off the same every time. For series production.

The drive is the other choice. Hydraulic benders give the force for large pipe. All-electric servo benders are faster, quieter and more precise, and switch parts quickly, which suits small and medium tube in series work.

Tooling, springback and what to send us

Each tube size and each CLR needs its own tooling: the bend die, the clamp die, the pressure die, and for mandrel bending the mandrel and the wiper die. Every bender comes with one set in the size you ask for, and CNC benders can carry stacked tools for several radii or sizes in one part. Springback, the angle the tube opens up after the bend, grows with the strength of the material and the radius. The NC or CNC compensates for it once it has been measured on the first part.
