What does a tube end former do?

A tube end former changes the shape of the end of a tube so it can be joined to another part without welding, or with an easier weld:

  • Expanding opens the end so the next tube slips inside: exhaust slip joints, telescoping handrail, sleeves.
  • Reducing (swaging) closes the end so it fits into the next tube, a muffler or a hose.
  • Beading rolls or presses a raised ring for a clamp, a hose or a seal.
  • Flaring and flanging turn the end outward for a fitting or a bolted joint.
  • Calibrating sizes the end exactly round for an accurate fit.
  • Grooving rolls a groove for O-rings and quick couplings.

Exhaust and muffler, ventilation and chimney, furniture, automotive, HVAC and hydraulic-line manufacturers all use these machines, often right beside the bender or the saw.

Ram, inner/outer or rotary forming?

  • Ram forming. The end is pushed into, or over, a forming tool by a hydraulic or servo ram. It is the simplest way to expand and reduce. Single-station machines such as the ISITAN SFF do one operation per stroke, and multi-station machines such as the CSM EF carry several tools on one ram, so expanding, reducing, beading and flaring run in sequence without a tool change.
  • Inner/outer (IO) forming. A tool inside the tube and closing jaws or radial pistons around it form the end between them, in one stroke. Shapes come out more precise and more complex: beads, flanges, embossing and calibration in any position on round or shaped tube.
  • Rotary forming. Turning rollers form beads, grooves and flares while the tube or the head rotates. It is the method for clean beads and grooves without marking the tube.

How to size the machine

The forming force grows with the tube diameter, the wall thickness and the strength of the material, and with how far the end is expanded or reduced. A machine is rated for a range of tube OD and a maximum wall in mild steel, for example expanding Ø1-3/8″ to Ø3-1/2″ tube in 14 ga on the SFF 90-1. Stainless and thicker walls need more force and are checked case by case. Larger changes in diameter may need two steps to avoid splitting or wrinkling the end. For production, look at the cycle time and at how the tube is loaded. A machine that clamps, forms and releases in a few seconds, with the operator loading the next tube, keeps up with a bender.

Tooling, change-over and drive

Each end shape and each tube size needs its own tooling: an expanding cone and segments, reducing jaws, bead rolls or IO tools. Ask what the tooling for your parts costs and how long a change takes. On good machines it takes a few minutes with quick-change holders, or none at all on multi-station machines. On the drive:

  • Hydraulic machines offer the most force for the price.
  • All-electric (servo) machines use less energy, run quieter, need no oil, and control the force and position directly.
