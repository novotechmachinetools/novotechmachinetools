Why cut rebar with a machine?

Every bar in a concrete structure has to be cut to the length on the bar list before it is bent and placed. On a construction schedule, time and precision decide the cost. Cutting by hand with a cut-off saw or a disc grinder is slow, throws sparks and sharp edges, and the lengths drift. A rebar cutter shears a bar in one stroke, and most cutters take several bars at once. The machine brings five things.

More cuts per hour

Hand cutting is slow and hard physical work. On a cutter the operator feeds the bar to the stop and steps on the pedal. A hydraulic cutter shears it in a few seconds, and a mechanical cutter in under a second. A day's bar list is cut in an hour or two, and the crew spends the time it saves on bending, tying and placing.

Fewer injuries

Cutting with a disc means sparks, dust, noise, flying fragments and a hot, sharp cut end, and holding heavy bar against a saw strains the back and arms. On a cutter the blades do the work inside a guarded head. The operator works the machine by pedal or lever, away from the blades, with an emergency stop within reach.

Lengths that match the bar list

A bar cut short does not lap or anchor as the engineer designed, and a bar cut long does not fit the form. With a length stop the cutter repeats the same length bar after bar, so every bar fits the cage. A sheared end is square and clean, ready for bending or threading.

Lower cost and less scrap

One operator does the work of several, with no abrasive discs to buy. Bars cut to the right length the first time mean less scrap. You can cut in your own yard instead of waiting, and paying, for a fabricator.

One machine for many bar sizes

A project uses bars of many diameters. A cutter shears everything from the smallest bar to its rated maximum, one bar at a time or several smaller bars together, with no change of tooling.

Types of rebar cutters

  • Manual rebar cutters — hand shears and lever cutters — cost little and need no power, but they cut only small bars, slowly, with the operator's strength. They are for a few bars a day.
  • Portable electric-hydraulic cutters run on 110 or 220 V single-phase and are light enough to carry to the job site and cut where the bar is placed. See the POWER 24.
  • Hydraulic rebar cutters have the strength for heavy bar and the versatility for sites and shops. They cost more than mechanical cutters and give a smooth, quiet cut, stop mid-stroke and need little maintenance. Single-acting cutters with spring return are the standard rebar cutter for sites and small shops. See the H series. Double-acting cutters are built for the rebar shop and precast plant and cut a single bar up to Ø2-3/8″ (60 mm). See the SH series.
  • Mechanical rebar cutters drive the blade from a flywheel and cut every 0.8 seconds. They are for a shop that cuts all day. See the M series.
  • Combined bender-cutters put a cutter and a bending disc on one frame, with one power connection and one operator. See the MAX 40.
  • Mesh cutters and automatic shear lines — the MH 8C shears welded mesh, and the automatic shear lines cut whole bundles to programmed lengths when a shop cuts tonnes a day.

Capacity and size

Start with the largest bar you cut and its grade. A cutter's capacity is rated for one bar of a given diameter in a given steel strength, and it drops for stronger bar. The capacity table also gives how many smaller bars the machine cuts at once. Small cutters for residential and site work cut bar up to about Ø1″ (25 mm). Industrial cutters for the rebar shop go to Ø2-3/8″ (60 mm). Choose a cutter that cuts the bars you use most with some margin, rather than one that is always at its limit. The nameplate on every GMS machine shows the capacities for each steel strength. The rebar bender and cutter buying guide lists Canadian bar sizes from 10M to 55M and explains which capacity column fits 400W and 500W bar.

Check the size of the machine as well as its capacity. It has to fit the shop or the yard, with room for the bar on both sides of the blades and for a length stop.

Power supply

Cutters are powered by electricity, by hydraulics driven by an electric motor, or by hand. The choice depends on the size of your projects, the power available where the cutting is done, and how many bars you cut. Look at the supply you have before you choose a size:

  • Single-phase 110 or 220 V — the portable cutter plugs into a site panel, a generator or a shop outlet. It suits residential and small commercial work.
  • Three-phase 600 V — the H, SH and M cutters for rebar shops need three-phase power, 600 V in most Canadian plants (208, 240 or 480 V on request). Make sure your building has it before you choose a stationary machine.

Stack cutting

Stack cutting means shearing several bars together in one stroke. The capacity table of every GMS cutter gives how many bars of each size it cuts at once. Stack cutting brings:

  • Efficiency — several bars in the time of one, which shortens the cutting of a bar list.
  • Precision — the bars in the stack are cut against the same stop, to the same length, with the same square end.
  • Lower labour cost — fewer strokes and fewer hours per tonne.
  • Less waste — bars cut to the right length together mean fewer offcuts and rejects.
  • Productivity and a competitive edge — more tonnes per shift, faster turnaround and better prices for your customers.

Hardened blades and parts

The blades take the full force of every cut. Hardened blades and parts bring:

  • Durability — hardened steel resists wear and the blades last longer.
  • No deformation — hardened blades and blade holders keep their shape under heavy bar.
  • No breakage — they are tough enough not to chip or crack in heavy-duty cutting.
  • Consistent cuts — sharp blades that keep their shape give clean, square cuts.
  • A lower cost over time — they cost more at first and much less over the life of the machine.

Construction sites and rebar shops are hard on machines, so choose a cutter built from good materials, by a maker with a record of reliability. Check that spare blades, parts and service are available in Canada. NovoTech stocks the blades and parts of the GMS cutters in Mississauga, ON, Canada.

What to look for inside the machine

A CSA-approved electrical panel

In Canada every machine has to be inspected by a CSA-accredited inspection body and carry its label. It matters for five reasons:

  • Safety standards — the CSA codes set strict safety criteria and reduce the risk of accidents and electrical hazards.
  • Operator protection — the operator works on equipment that has been inspected.
  • No electric shock — the label confirms that the electrical components meet the safety regulations.
  • Peace of mind — for operators and employers alike.
  • Legal compliance — the CSA label is usually a legal requirement, and a machine without it can bring costly penalties.

Known electrical components

Contactors, relays, overload protection and drives from Siemens, Schneider Electric, Eaton and ABB:

  • Reliability — the electrical system works smoothly, day after day.
  • Current technology — efficient, well-made components in the panel.
  • Safety — built to strict standards, with less risk of electrical faults.
  • Long life — made for harsh industrial conditions, with less downtime.
  • Parts everywhere — sold and supported worldwide, so replacement parts are easy to find in Canada.

Motor, hydraulics and drive

On a hydraulic cutter an electric motor drives the hydraulic pump, and on a mechanical cutter a motor drives the flywheel through a gearbox. Heavy-duty industrial components bring:

  • Durability — they are made for heavy loads and continuous work.
  • Consistent performance — the same force on every stroke, so the cuts stay clean.
  • Productivity — they take demanding workloads all day.
  • Little maintenance — less repair work and downtime.

Motor overload protection

A thermal overload relay, or a variable-frequency inverter drive, detects excessive current or temperature in the motor and cuts the power before an oversized bar or a jam can damage it:

  • No motor damage — the motor is stopped before it overheats or is overloaded.
  • Longer machine life — fewer costly repairs and replacements.
  • Operator safety — no overheated motor failing during the work.
  • Less downtime — problems are stopped before they halt a project.
  • Efficiency — an inverter drive also sets the motor speed and power to the load, saving energy and wear, and its settings can be adjusted to the machine.
  • Compliance — in Canada, motor overload protection is required by the safety regulations and the CSA codes.
  • Cost savings — lower maintenance costs, no equipment damage, and no liability from accidents.

Safety features

Look for a guard over the blades, a foot pedal or hand lever, and emergency stop buttons within reach of the operator.

Portable or stationary?

Portability decides where you can use the machine.

Single-phase portable cutters, for bar up to about Ø1″ (25 mm):

  • Light — small and compact, easy to carry and move around the site.
  • Easy to move — one person or a small crew moves them, and they fit in the back of a pickup or a van.
  • Used where the bar is placed — they cut on site, near the work, with less handling of the bar.

Three-phase industrial cutters, for bar up to Ø2-3/8″ (60 mm):

  • Heavy — far too heavy and bulky to move by hand. The H series rolls on wheels around the shop or the yard.
  • Moved with a forklift or crane — the heavy machines are placed once and usually stay in the shop.
  • Made for the shop — for rebar shops and plants that process tonnes of bar a day, not for moving from site to site.

Budget: the cost per tonne, not the price tag

The price matters, but a cheap machine that stops in the middle of a pour costs more than it saved. Look at:

  • Long-term value — a well-built cutter costs more at first and saves it back in uptime, maintenance and scrap.
  • The features you need — the capacity, speed and length stop your work calls for, without paying for what it does not.
  • Quality — a reputable maker and solid construction. Low-priced machines often lack the durability for heavy construction work and cost more in repairs and replacement.
  • Operator efficiency — features that make the operator faster pay back in labour and finish projects sooner.
  • Warranty and support — a solid warranty and blades, parts and service in Canada.
  • Resale value — quality cutters from known makers keep their value.
