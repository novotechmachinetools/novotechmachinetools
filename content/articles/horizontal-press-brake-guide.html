What can a horizontal press brake do?

A horizontal press brake is a hydraulic press laid on its side. The ram pushes the tool across a heavy steel table at bench height, so bar stock lies flat in front of the operator. Long parts rest on the table, the operator watches the bend from the side, and tools change in minutes. With the right tooling, one machine does all of this:

  • bends flat bar flat-wise and on edge (the hard way), and bends angle, channel, solid bar and square tube
  • bends pipe without a mandrel, and reduces or expands pipe ends
  • punches, shears and notches bar
  • straightens shafts, bar and sections between support pins
  • shears, punches, bends and twists copper and aluminum busbar

That is why it is the workhorse of maintenance and repair shops, gate, railing and fence makers, trailer and agricultural fabricators, and switchgear builders.

How much force do I need?

Size the press by its heaviest job, which is usually one of three:

  • Bending flat bar the hard way. This is how horizontal presses are rated, in the widest and thickest mild-steel flat bar they bend on edge. As a guide, the HP 20 takes 7-7/8 × 3/8″, the HP 60 takes 11-13/16 × 5/8″ and the HP 120 / 150 take 19-11/16 × 1-3/16″ and 1-9/16″.
  • Punching. The force in US tons is about hole diameter × material thickness × 80 for mild steel, in inches. A Ø13/16″ hole in 1/2″ plate takes about 32 tons, so an HP 40 or larger.
  • Straightening. Heavy shafts and sections need the long stroke, the hardened support columns and the T-slot table of the HP 120 and HP 150.

Bending flat bar flat-wise takes far less force: it works like a press brake on a narrow strip, so a 4 × 1/2″ bar in a 4″ V takes about 12 tons. Stainless steel needs about 1.5 times the force of mild steel. Leave headroom for the occasional heavier job: a press that punches your largest hole at its limit wears faster.

Which tools do I need?

A horizontal press does only what its tooling lets it do, so choose the machine and the tools together. Start from your parts:

  • Brackets and clamps. A radius punch with a V-die, or a press brake punch with a multi-V die.
  • U-bolts, hooks and U-shapes. A spring-loaded U-bending tool.
  • Railing and gate parts. Profile and round-bar bending tools, and wide-radius tools.
  • Pipe. A mandrel-less pipe bending set for each pipe size, tools for solid bar and thick-wall pipe, and end reduction, expansion and notching tools.
  • Holes and cuts in bar. Punch and die sets and shearing blades.
  • Busbar. Shearing, punching, flat, edge and Z-bending, corner notching and twisting tools.

If no standard tool fits, a die can be designed and made for your part from the drawing. Budget for the tooling together with the machine.

NC control, recipes and the back gauge

The NC sets the start and end of the stroke, so the bend angle and the punch depth repeat from part to part. In manual mode the ram works between those limits only while the pedal is pressed, which is how you set up and make single parts. The automatic mode repeats the cycle for series work. In recipe mode, a part with several bends or holes is stored as a list of positions and run step by step.

The servo NC back gauge, 3.3 to 9.8 ft long, positions the bar for every step of a recipe. Its pull-back moves the stop away during the bend, so a part that swings toward the gauge does not push it off position. If you make the same multi-bend parts every week, the back gauge pays for itself in the first batches.

Safety, power and installation

Set the stroke limits as tight as the job allows, keep hands out of the tool area with the pedal control and guards, and train every operator on tool changes with the ram retracted.
