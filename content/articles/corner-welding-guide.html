What does a corner welding machine do?

Boxes, cabinets, trays, enclosures and doors bent from sheet have to be welded at their corners. By hand, every corner depends on the welder: the speed varies, the heat builds up, thin sheet warps and the weld then has to be ground and dressed. A corner welding machine drives the TIG or plasma torch along the seam at a set speed, while the part is held square against copper that draws off the heat. The operator places the part with its corner against the copper squaring guide, locks it with a button and presses start, then prepares the next part while the machine welds. Every corner comes out straight, even and the same, and needs less grinding afterwards.

1, 2, 3 or 4 axes?

The number of axes decides what kind of corner the machine welds in one cycle. One axis welds a straight corner seam, the corner of a plain box: up to 7-7/8″ high on the CWS 200, 11-13/16″ on the CWS 300 and 15-3/4″ on the WELCOR 400 X. On two axes, X and Y, the torch is driven in two directions; the WELCOR 400 XY welds seams up to 15-3/4″ on X and 4-5/16″ on Y. Three axes add Z, up to 1-15/16″, so a seam that runs in three directions is welded in one cycle. The four-axis WELCOR adds torch rotation, from vertical to about 120°, and a laser beside the torch that checks the edges and memorizes the path before it welds, so it finds the corners by itself. Send us a drawing of your part and we will tell you how many axes it needs.

Why the copper matters

The part is pressed from inside against copper: on the CWS and the WELCOR 400 up to three axes an outer unit 7-7/8″ or 11-13/16″ high and an inner unit from 1-15/16″ to 11-13/16″ chosen for the corner; on the four-axis WELCOR modular aluminum and copper pads from 3/8″ to 7-7/8″ thick, matched to the box. The copper holds the corner square while it is welded, which matters more than anything for a good-looking box. It also draws the heat away, so thin sheet does not warp and stainless does not discolour as much. Cooled inner groups are recommended to avoid dark marks on the parts. Ask for them if you weld stainless every day.

How to bend the corner

The machine welds what the press brake gives it. The two edges should meet on the corner, .0004″ (0.01 mm) apart and centred on it. Where the bend does not allow it, they may overlap by half the sheet thickness at most; on the CWS the gap between the edges must not exceed .008″ (0.2 mm). A consistent bend radius and square flanges pay off at the welder.

Which parts fit?

Check three sizes against your parts: the smallest and largest box, the corner height and the material. The CWS 200, CWS 300 and CWS 300 XY take parts from 3-15/16 × 3-15/16″ to 59-1/16 × 59-1/16″ on a 32-5/16″ square work table (39-3/8″ to order); the WELCOR 400 takes parts to 39-3/8 × 39-3/8″, and on the four-axis the smallest box is 1-15/16″ high. Mild steel, stainless and aluminum are all welded.

TIG or plasma, and the power source

TIG is the usual choice for thin sheet and for the cleanest-looking welds on stainless and aluminum. Plasma welding is faster, with deeper penetration and a more focused arc, and suits longer production runs. The machines weld at 0 to 47 in/min with a TIG or a plasma source, which is not included: GECAM offers the Lorch T 250, the DC version for mild and stainless steel and the AC/DC version for aluminum as well, with a cooling unit and a torch with a 9′10″ cable. Tell us the material, thickness and corner you weld, and we will quote the machine with its power source and set up the process.

Installation

The corner welder stands on a level floor near the press brake, with shielding gas and the welding power source beside it, and fume extraction as for any welding. It runs on 600 V three-phase with a CSA-approved panel (208, 240 or 480 V on request). NovoTech installs it, sets up the process on your first parts and trains your operators. A corner grinder next to it finishes the welded corners to a flush or radiused edge for painting or polishing.
