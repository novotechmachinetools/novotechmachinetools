Why deburr and round the edges?

Laser cutting, punching, shearing and plasma cutting all leave something on the edge of a part: a burr, dross or slag, or simply a sharp square edge. A burr cuts hands and gloves, keeps parts from lying flat in the press brake, and spoils welds and fits. On a sharp edge, paint and powder coating pull thin as they cure, so the edge is the first place a coated part rusts. Coating standards for corrosion protection, such as ISO 12944, ask for sharp edges to be rounded to a radius of at least 2 mm (5/64″). A deburring machine does all of this in one pass on a conveyor, where hand grinding takes minutes per part and never comes out the same twice.

Belt, brushes or both?

  • Abrasive belt. A wide belt on a rubber contact roller cuts the burr off and levels the surface. It is the tool for heavy burrs and for a flat, even face.
  • Rotating and oscillating brushes. Top brushes and brush carousels round the edges all around the part, inside holes and slots too, and give the radius a coating needs.
  • Scotch-Brite brushes. Cylindrical non-woven brushes give an even satin grain on stainless and aluminum.

A typical job-shop machine combines a belt, top brushes and a finishing brush, so it deburrs, rounds and finishes in one pass. Parts cut with plasma or oxy-fuel need a machine that breaks the slag off the bottom edge first.

Width, thickness and small parts

Choose the working width from the widest part you run, plus a margin: 6 – 12″ for small laser and punched parts, about 25″ for most job-shop work, and 43 – 53″ for nested parts or full sheets. Thickness is rarely the limit, since these machines take parts several inches thick, but the minimum part size is. A vacuum table holds small parts of any material on the conveyor, and a magnetic conveyor holds small steel parts. Very small or awkward parts are better finished on a brush workstation.

Dust, wet or dry, and running costs

A dry machine needs a dust collector sized for its airflow, from about 1,200 CFM on the small machines to 3,500 CFM on a 53″ machine. We quote a matching collector with the machine. Never run aluminum and steel through the same dry collector: mixed aluminum and steel dust is a fire and explosion hazard. For aluminum, or for mixed materials, a wet machine is the safer choice. It sprays water on every tool, takes the dust to a sludge tank and keeps the parts cool, so they do not deform. Running costs are mostly abrasives: belts, brushes and Scotch-Brite rolls. The right grit sequence for your parts makes them last longer, and we set it up when the machine is installed.
