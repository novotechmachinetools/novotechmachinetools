What does a belt grinder do in a fabrication shop?

A belt grinder takes the burr off a cut, blends a weld, squares the end of a bar, puts a chamfer on an edge for welding and grinds a radius, faster than a hand grinder and with a flatter, more even result. The belt is long, so it runs cooler and lasts longer than a disc or wheel of the same grit. A grinder works in three ways:

  • On the platen. Flat grinding and squaring, against a flat plate behind the belt.
  • On the contact wheel. Aggressive stock removal, radii and tube notching. A hard wheel cuts fast, and a soft wheel blends and finishes.
  • On the slack belt. Between the wheels, for contours and blending.

Belt width and motor power

  • 2″ belts (2 × 72″, 2 × 79″) are for knives, tools and detail work.
  • 3″ belts are the all-round width for deburring and blending in a fabrication shop.
  • 4″ and 6″ belts grind wider parts flat and last longer.
  • 8″ belts are for flat sanding of wide parts and for radius grinding.

Power is what keeps the belt cutting under pressure. About 3 – 4 HP on a 3″ belt is a good production grinder, and heavy stock removal on wide belts wants 5 HP or more. A grinder that slows down when you lean on it glazes its belts and wastes time. Most shops start with a 3″ or 6″ pedestal grinder and add a disc sander for flat faces and accurate angles.

Belt speed and abrasives

Run steel and stainless at about 5,000 – 6,500 ft/min with zirconia or ceramic belts. Aluminum, heat-sensitive parts and fine finishing need a slower speed, about 2,500 – 4,000 ft/min, which is why a two-speed or variable-speed grinder is worth having. Choose the abrasive for the job:

  • Aluminum oxide. General-purpose belts for light work and mild steel.
  • Zirconia. Tough belts for steel under heavy pressure.
  • Ceramic. They cut cool and last longest on stainless and hard steels.
  • Non-woven. Surface-conditioning belts for a satin finish.

Work through the grits, for example 36 or 40 for stock removal, then 60 – 80, then 120 or finer for the finish. Do not jump straight from coarse to fine. Keep separate belts for stainless and carbon steel, because iron particles from a steel belt cause rust spots on stainless.

Disc sanders, bench grinders and chamfering machines

  • Disc sanders. They give a flat face and an accurate angle against their table: square ends, weld chamfers, flat parts. Combination machines put a belt and a disc on one stand.
  • Bench grinders and wire-wheel machines. For tool sharpening, wire brushing and small parts.
  • Chamfering machines and end grinders. They put a consistent weld bevel or chamfer on plate edges, tube and bar ends, which is faster and more even than a hand grinder for repeat work.

Dust and safety

Grinding dust settles everywhere and does not belong in anyone's lungs. Connect each grinder to a dust collector made for metal. Sparks reach the filter, so never share it with a woodworking machine, and never grind aluminum and steel into the same dry collector: the mixed dust is a fire and explosion hazard. On bench grinders, keep the work rest within 1/8″ of the wheel and the upper guard within 1/4″, as grinder safety standards call for. Use only wheels rated for the grinder's speed, and wear eye and face protection.
