The five things that define a band saw blade

To order a blade, or compare two, you need five things:

  1. Width. Set by the saw's wheels and guides, for example 1″ on most 10 – 13″ shop saws, 1-1/4″ and 1-1/2″ on mid-size automatic saws, and 2″ to 3-1/8″ on double-column production saws. A wider blade cuts straighter under higher feed, but only if the saw is built for it.
  2. Thickness. It goes with the width: 0.035″ on 1″ blades, 0.042″ on 1-1/4″, 0.050″ on 1-1/2″, and 0.063″ on 2″ and wider. Do not fit a thicker blade than the saw's wheels are made for: it cracks from bending fatigue.
  3. Length. The loop length of your machine. Find it in the manual, on the old blade's label, or send us the saw make and model.
  4. Tooth pitch. In teeth per inch (TPI), for example 3/4 TPI variable pitch. See the next section.
  5. Tooth material and form. Bimetal M42 for general work, carbide-tipped for production cutting of solids and hard material, and a tooth form and set made for the job (for example a strong, positive-rake tooth for solids).

Written as one line, an order reads like: 1″ × 0.035″ × 12′ 6″, 4/6 TPI, M42.

Choosing the pitch from the contact length

The pitch depends on the contact length, the length of material the blade is in at any moment:

  • for a solid bar, its diameter or width
  • for tube and profiles, the walls the blade cuts through, which change as the cut goes down
  • for a bundle, the walls of every piece in the cut

Short contact lengths need fine teeth, so that several teeth share the cut and none of them catches on a thin wall. Long contact lengths need coarse teeth, with gullets big enough to carry the chips out of the cut. As a guide, thin-wall tube and small profiles take 10/14 TPI, bar of 2 to 4″ takes 3/4 or 2/3 TPI, and large solids 1.4/2 TPI or coarser. The table of each blade on this page gives the contact length every pitch is meant for.

A variable pitch (two tooth spacings on one blade, such as 3/4) runs quieter and resists vibration, which makes it the best choice for profiles, tube and bundles. A constant pitch suits uniform solid material.

Breaking in a new blade

The tips of new teeth are very sharp, and a full feed in the first cuts chips them. The blade then never reaches its normal life. Run a new blade at the normal blade speed but about half the normal feed for the first 50 – 100 square inches of cut, then raise the feed in steps to normal. The few minutes this takes are repaid many times over in blade life. Carbide blades need the same care.

Making blades last

  • Tension. Tension the blade to the value the blade maker gives, using a tension gauge or the saw's tension indicator, not by feel. Too little tension gives crooked cuts, and carbide blades need more tension than bimetal.
  • Guides. Set the guides as close to the work as the material allows, and keep the guide carbides and the chip brush in good condition. A worn chip brush lets chips be carried back into the cut, and they break teeth.
  • Speed and feed. Run the blade speed and feed for the material: slower for stainless and tool steel, faster for aluminum. Watch the chips: curled and silver or light gold is right. Blue chips mean too much speed or feed, and fine dust means the feed is too light and the teeth are rubbing.
  • Coolant. Use coolant at the correct concentration, flooding the cut.
  • Slack. Release the tension at the end of the shift on saws where the maker recommends it.
