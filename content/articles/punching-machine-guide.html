How much tonnage does a hole take?

A hydraulic punch shears a slug out of the plate in one stroke. The force needed depends on the length of the cut edge and the thickness. For mild steel (about 65,000 psi tensile), in US tons and inches:

  • Round hole. Diameter × thickness × 80. A Ø1″ hole in 1/2″ plate takes 1 × 0.5 × 80 = 40 tons.
  • Oblong and shaped holes. Perimeter × thickness × 25. A 13/16 × 1-1/2″ slot in 1/2″ plate (perimeter about 3.9″) takes about 49 tons.

For other materials, multiply the mild-steel figure: about 1.5 for stainless steel, about 0.4 for aluminum. Leave about 20 % headroom for harder plate and for wear.

There is also a limit set by the punch itself. Do not punch a hole smaller in diameter than the plate is thick in mild steel, or smaller than about twice the thickness in stainless. The punch is loaded like a column and breaks before it pierces. For thin plate and large holes, a large-hole tool punches up to about Ø4-7/8″ in 3/16″.

Throat depth, table and die clearance

The throat depth is the distance from the punch centre to the back of the frame. It sets how far from the edge of a plate a hole can be. A deep throat (24.6″ on the HPM 65 to 175) lets the machine punch the centre holes of base plates and wide gussets. Check it against the widest plate on which you punch an inside hole.

The die is larger than the punch by the clearance, about 10 % of the plate thickness in total for mild steel. For 5/8″ plate that is a die about 1/16″ larger than the punch. Stainless needs a little more clearance. The right clearance gives a clean hole with a small burr and a long punch life. Too tight a clearance overloads the punch, and too loose a clearance rolls a large burr. Keep a few die sizes for each punch if you punch a range of thicknesses.

A stripper holds the plate down as the punch withdraws. It should sit close to the top of the plate, so that a hand cannot fit between the stripper and the plate.

Manual, NC or CNC?

  • Manual punch with a back gauge. For single holes and short runs: mark or gauge the plate, position it by hand and punch.
  • Double-head punch. Two operators can work at once, or one operator can keep two tool sizes ready without a change.
  • CNC table. The CNC table moves the plate under the punch to programmed positions (±0.004″ on the HPM CNC), so a hole pattern programmed once repeats on every part. It is the machine for steel-construction plates where every hole must line up with the mating member.
  • NC punching line. An NC feeding table pushes angle, flat bar and beams along the punch and copies the hole pattern. It is the economical alternative to a CNC angle line for bracing, towers and ladders.

Punching machine or ironworker?

If holes are most of your work (base plates, gussets, clips and flat-bar brackets), a dedicated punch is the faster machine: a deeper throat, a free table at punching height, and a frame that can carry a CNC table. If you also shear angle and bar and notch corners every day, an ironworker puts those stations on the same frame, and the punches and dies are the same on both. Many structural shops run both: the ironworker for cutting and the punch for holes.

Safety and installation

Align the punch and the die with the ram at the bottom of its stroke and the machine stopped. A punch that hits the die edge does not bend: it chips or shatters. Keep the stripper and the guards set for the plate in the machine. In Ontario, a new punching machine needs a pre-start health and safety review under Regulation 851 before it runs; NovoTech prepares the documentation.
