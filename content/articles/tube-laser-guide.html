What does a tube laser replace?

Cutting a frame part from tube the traditional way takes several machines and steps:

  • sawing the tube to length
  • laying out the holes, then drilling and slotting them
  • notching or coping the ends for tube-to-tube joints
  • deburring
  • fixturing it all for welding

A tube laser does all of these in one pass, straight from the 3D model. The parts also fit better, because every hole and cope is cut in the same set-up.

The real saving is often at the welding table. Tabs and slots cut by the laser let the parts of a frame lock together, so fewer jigs are needed and the welder spends less time fitting. To judge a tube laser for your shop, take your ten most common tube parts and count the operations and handling each one gets today. The more operations per part, the faster the machine pays.

Which size tube laser do I need?

Compare machines on four numbers:

  • Chuck range. The smallest and largest tube the chucks hold, round and square. Most railing, furniture, frame and trailer work falls between about Ø3/4″ and Ø8″, which is why the 8 – 9″ chuck is the most common size. Structural tube and pipe need 14″ chucks, and the heavy machines take 25″.
  • Bar length. 20, 29.5 or 39 ft (6, 9 or 12 m). Buy the length your tube comes in, so bars are not cut before loading.
  • Bar weight. The heaviest bar the chucks and supports carry, in pounds per foot or per bar.
  • Laser power. 1.5 – 3 kW cuts the thin and medium walls of most railing, furniture and frame tube. Walls to about 1/2″ need about 12 kW, and 3/4″ about 20 kW.

Two, three or four chucks — and the tail

Two chucks hold the bar at both ends, and this is the machine most shops need. With two chucks, the last part of the bar cannot be cut close to the chuck, so a remnant (the tail) is left over: from a few inches to more than a foot, depending on the machine. A third chuck in the middle takes over the bar as it gets shorter, so the last part is cut with little or no tail. On expensive stainless or heavy tube, that saving adds up quickly. Three and four chucks on heavy machines also carry long, heavy bars without sag, for structural sections and towers.

Loading, profiles, bevels and weld seams

  • Loading. An automatic bundle loader separates one bar from the bundle, measures it and feeds it to the chuck, so one operator runs the machine while the next bundle is prepared. Small machines can be loaded by hand. Plan the unloading as well: finished parts drop onto a table or conveyor and must be sorted. Long parts need an unloading length to match.
  • Profiles. Round, square, rectangular and oval tube, and open profiles such as angle, channel and flat bar. Open profiles need chucks and supports made for them. Heavy lines also take H-beams.
  • Bevels. A bevel head tilts the beam, typically to ±45°, for weld preparations and true mitred corners. Without it the cut is square to the tube wall.
  • Weld seams. On welded tube, seam detection turns the tube so the seam is never where a hole or a cope is cut.

Software, space and power

The CNC software imports STEP or IGES models of the tube parts, unfolds them onto the profile, nests them along the bar to use as much of it as possible, and writes the program from the cutting database. Offline programming on a PC lets the office prepare the next job while the machine cuts.

Plan the floor before you order. With the loader and unloader, a 20 ft (6 – 7 m) machine needs about 65 ft of length and a 39 ft (12 m) machine about 100 ft. The laser needs 600 V three-phase power (through the transformer we supply) or 480 V, the chiller that comes with it, assist gas and a dust collector. Keep the building heated so the chiller never freezes.
