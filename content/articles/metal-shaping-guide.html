Which shaping machine does what?

Auto body and hot-rod builders, restoration shops, motorcycle and aircraft fabricators and metal artists shape sheet into compound curves with a small family of machines. Each does one job best:

  • English wheel. It stretches the sheet between a large upper wheel and a small lower anvil wheel as the panel is rolled back and forth. It makes smooth, gradual compound curves: fenders, hoods, roofs, tanks, aircraft skins.
  • Planishing hammer. Fast, light blows smooth the panel after shaping and take out hammer and wheel marks.
  • Power hammer. Heavier blows, with shaping dies, raise, stretch and shrink the panel. It shapes as well as planishes.
  • Shrinker-stretcher. It gathers or spreads the metal of a flange so it curves, for window frames, wheel arches, curved brackets and angle.
  • Roller former. It embosses patterns, such as diamonds, into sheet.
  • Air forging hammer. A self-contained hammer for hot forging bar and billet in the forge.

Most shaping shops start with an English wheel and a shrinker-stretcher, then add a planishing or power hammer.

Choosing an English wheel

  • Throat depth. The distance from the wheels to the back of the frame limits how far into a panel you can work. 18 – 24″ suits motorcycle tanks, fenders and repair panels, 28 – 30″ handles most car panels, and 40 – 45″ takes hoods, roofs and aircraft skins.
  • Frame stiffness. The frame must not spring open under pressure, or the wheel loses its grip on the panel and shaping becomes guesswork. A heavy welded frame matters more than any other feature.
  • Anvil wheels. A set of lower wheels from nearly flat to tightly crowned lets you choose how fast and where the panel stretches. A quick-release lever opens the wheels without losing the setting.
  • Capacity. Heavy wheels such as the F1.5 and F2.0 take 16 and 14 ga steel, and lighter wheels 18 – 22 ga. Aluminum is easier to wheel than steel of the same thickness.

Planishing hammer or power hammer?

A pneumatic planishing hammer runs on shop air and delivers many light blows a second between a top die and an anvil. It is the tool to smooth a panel after the wheel, and to make small corrections. An electric power hammer, with variable speed and more force, shapes as well as planishes. With shaping dies it shrinks, stretches and raises panels that would take hours by hand. Compare the throat, the force or stroke, the speed range and the dies included, and check that dies are easy to change. Many shops have both: the power hammer to shape, the planishing hammer to finish.

Shrinker-stretchers and forging hammers

A shrinker-stretcher grips a flange between serrated jaws. The shrinking jaws push the metal together, so the flange curves toward it. The stretching jaws pull it apart, so it curves the other way. Shrink and stretch on the same part to make S-curves, without cutting and welding. Foot-operated machines leave both hands to guide the work, a deeper throat reaches further into a panel, and two-head machines save changing jaws. Check the capacity for the material, typically 16 – 18 ga steel and heavier aluminum.

An air forging hammer is sized by the weight of its ram, which sets the size of the stock it can forge. It needs a solid concrete foundation, separate from the shop floor, to absorb the blows.
