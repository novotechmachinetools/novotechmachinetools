Cold saw, abrasive saw or band saw?

All three cut bar, tube and profiles to length, but they leave very different ends.

  • Cold saw. A toothed circular blade turns slowly under coolant and leaves a square, burr-free end that is cool to the touch and ready to weld or fit. It cuts tube and profiles in seconds, and the blade is resharpened many times.
  • Abrasive saw. A cut-off wheel grinds through the steel at high speed. The saw costs the least, but the end is hot, burred and slightly bevelled, sparks fly, and the wheels are thrown away as they wear down.
  • Band saw. A band saw cuts large solids, wide sections and bundles that no cold saw can take, with a low blade cost per cut. On small tube and profiles it is slower per cut than a cold saw.

The usual choice in a fabrication shop: a cold saw for the many cuts in tube and profiles up to about 4″ where the finish and the angle matter, and a band saw for big material.

HSS or carbide blades?

HSS blades run slowly, typically 45 – 90 rpm on a 10 – 14″ blade, with flood coolant. They are the standard blade for tube, thin walls, profiles and mixed work, and they are resharpened dozens of times. Coated blades last longer in harder material: TiN for medium-hard steel, TiCN for high-strength steel and TiAlN for stainless.

Carbide (TCT) blades run several times faster and are the fastest way to cut solid steel bar in production, but they need a saw built for them: high spindle speed, a rigid head, servo feeding and steady clamping. They also cost more per blade and per resharpening, so they pay off in long runs.

Choose the tooth pitch so that at least two or three teeth are in the wall of a thin tube, and a coarser pitch for solid bar, so each tooth gap can hold its chip. On the ISITAN SFC saws the blade bore is 32 mm with two Ø11 mm pin holes, a standard that is easy to source in Canada.

Capacity and miter cuts

The blade diameter sets the capacity. Every saw has a chart of the largest round tube, square tube, rectangular profile and solid bar it cuts at 90° and at each miter angle. Capacity drops as the head swivels, so check your largest profile at the angle you cut it, not only at 90°. Most fabrication saws miter both ways, to 45° left and right or more. A vise that clamps close to the blade on both sides gives a clean, square cut, and a bundle vise cuts several tubes at once. Solid bar takes far longer to cut than tube of the same diameter, so if most of your cutting is solid bar above about 2″, a band saw or a carbide saw is the better machine.

Manual, semi-automatic or automatic?

  • Manual pull-down saw. The operator clamps the vise and pulls the head down by hand. It is the simplest and lowest-priced saw, for occasional cuts and short runs.
  • Pneumatic vise. The vise clamps with air at the pull of a lever, which saves time on every cut and holds short pieces better.
  • Semi-automatic saw. The head descends at a regulated rate from a foot switch or start button, then returns. It is for batches of the same part with consistent feed and blade life.
  • Automatic saw. A servo gripper or carriage feeds the bar, and the saw clamps, cuts and counts to a list of lengths and quantities, typically to about ±.004″. Some saws load bars from a magazine and run unattended.

Add roller stands or a roller table with a length stop on the infeed and outfeed side for long bars. They make even a manual saw much faster and more accurate.

Speed, coolant and installation

Two blade speeds, or an inverter with variable speed, let one saw cut steel at low speed and aluminum and copper faster. Stainless needs the low speed, a sharp coated blade and a steady feed: let the blade rub without cutting and stainless work-hardens. Keep the coolant at the concentration the maker gives. It cools the blade, flushes the chips and is the biggest single factor in blade life. Cold saws need little more than a solid floor and power: 600 V three-phase with a CSA-approved panel as standard (208, 240 or 480 V on request), and 240 V single-phase on the smallest models.
