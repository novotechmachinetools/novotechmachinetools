What is an ironworker?

An ironworker is a compact, multi-purpose hydraulic machine that punches, shears and notches structural steel — the workhorse of the fabrication shop, often called its Swiss Army knife. Ironworking is usually the first step in making a part, and one ironworker can cut and punch enough material to keep several welders and fitters busy. Modern machines generate their force with hydraulic cylinders driven by an electric motor.

Since the first machines of the late 1800s, the ironworker's strength has been the number of jobs it does on one frame:

  • Punching — round, oblong and square holes in plate, flat bar, angle and channel
  • Shearing — flat bar and plate, angle, round and square bar, and with special tooling channel, tee and tube
  • Notching — rectangular and V-notches in plate, flat bar and angle, and pipe notches
  • Bending — flat bar and short plate with press brake tooling

How to choose an ironworker

As versatile as an ironworker is, it is still easy to buy the wrong one — or at least not the best one for your work. These are the points we go through with customers before they buy.

Size: start with the tonnage

Choosing the right size of ironworker matters more than choosing the brand, and every manufacturer rates its machines in tons. Answer these questions first and you have the approximate capacity your shop needs:

  • What is the largest hole you punch, and in what thickness? That sets the punching tonnage.
  • What grade of steel do you punch and shear? Harder material needs more tonnage.
  • How far from the edge of the material is the farthest hole? That sets the throat depth.
  • What is the largest angle you shear, and how thick? That sets the angle-shear capacity.
  • What is the widest and thickest flat bar you shear? That sets the flat-bar shear capacity.

The main rating is the punching tonnage: the largest hole the machine punches through its thickest material. The shearing capacities for plate, angle and flat bar matter just as much, so compare them too. Most structural steel shops work in the 80 to 120 US ton range: 120 tons punches a 1-1/4″ hole through 1″ plate, shears 6 × 6 × 1/2″ angle and 12 × 1″ or 24 × 5/8″ flat bar in 55,000 psi steel. A smaller shop typically needs about 60 tons — enough to punch a 1-1/16″ hole through 5/8″ plate, shear 4 × 4″ angle and 10 × 5/8″ flat bar. Ornamental iron, welding and miscellaneous fabrication shops often work with machines of this size.

Not all tons are equal. A metric ton (2,205 lb) is heavier than a US ton (2,000 lb), so a machine rated at 80 metric tons should punch a larger hole than one rated at 80 US tons: about 1-1/8″ through 1″ mild steel against 1″ through 1″. Compare machines by the hole and thickness they punch, not only by the number on the nameplate.

Leave headroom above your largest everyday job — a machine that runs at full capacity all day wears out much sooner. Because steel grades and hardness vary, choose a machine about 20 % larger than your daily work requires, and add about 50 % to the tonnage for stainless steel.

Weight

Weight is not a goal in itself, but it shows how much steel went into the frame and ram — and a heavier frame and ram mean a stiffer machine that keeps its alignment and lasts longer.

Stations and tooling

Every ironworker should punch, shear plate, shear angle, shear round and square bar and notch: these are the jobs a fabricator repeats every day. Beyond them come plate bending, tube and bar bending, channel shears, large-hole punches, gang punching and other special tools.

On some machines the stations are built in — punch, angle shear, bar shear, notcher and flat-bar shear — and are always ready. Structural steel fabricators often prefer them, because the stations cover most of what they process with no tool changes. If you run a general welding, repair or job shop and do not know what the next customer will bring in, a machine with a tool table that takes a wider range of tooling may suit you better: press brake attachments, tube and channel shears, pipe and V-notchers, picket tools and special tooling — at the cost of some time to change from one job to the next.

Automation

An ironworker can be automated like any other machine tool. A simple electric back gauge makes repeat cuts accurate; a programmable gauge or positioning table spaces holes automatically; and some machines are built with CNC punching tables or NC feeding lines for angle and flat bar.

Flat-bar shear: fixed rake or scissors?

Every ironworker has a flat-bar shear; the stations differ in the length of the blade and in how it meets the material. A fixed-rake-angle (guillotine) shear keeps the same blade angle all along the cut, which can give more capacity without more tonnage — but because the rake angle cannot change, the drop piece always distorts the same amount. A scissors-type shear cuts thick material close to the pivot and thin material farther out, where the rake angle is flatter, so it distorts thin material less; scissors shears also tend to have longer blades, up to about 24″.

On some ironworkers the rake angle is set by adding or removing wedge-shaped shims above the blade. That takes skill and time, and if the shims are not reset each time the thickness changes, the machine can be damaged.

Safety

Look closely at the guarding. The punch guard (stripper) should adjust down to within 1/4″ of the top of the material, so the operator cannot put a hand between the material and the stripper, and every other station should be fully guarded too. Be careful with automatic urethane hold-downs: operators respect the blade but rarely expect to be hurt by a guard, and a badly set hold-down comes down with tons of force.

For safety and productivity, the machine should have an infinitely adjustable stroke control: a short stroke means fewer pinch points, more strokes per minute and better control when bending or using special tooling, where the upstroke must be set as well as the downstroke. Electric stroke controls react faster and stop more precisely than mechanical linkages, which only close the valve once the machine is already moving.

Train operators to align the punch and die. Punches are hardened to about 58 HRC, so a misaligned punch does not bend when it hits the die — it chips or shatters. The safest way is the one used on punch presses for decades: bring the ram to the bottom of its stroke, fit the punch and die with the punch already entered in the die, check the alignment and refit the guards, all without the machine moving.

Build quality

An ironworker transfers many tons of force through its pivot pins and beam, so look at the size of the pins and the section of the beam and side frames. Listen to the machine punch: a loud bang as the punch breaks through can mean the frame is stretching and snapping back, and over time that shock cracks welds. Better machines prevent it with heavier side frames, beams and pins.

Lubrication is another clue. Every machine has grease points on its pivots and guides, but a machine with 20 or more of them is usually fighting a galling problem — no operator will grease that many points every day. A central lubrication unit that feeds them all from one pump is a sign of a machine built to last.

Single-operator or dual-operator?

On a single-operator ironworker one cylinder serves every station, so one person works one station at a time. These machines are common in smaller shops and take less floor space. A dual-operator machine has a second cylinder: one powers the punch, the other the shearing and notching stations, so two people can work at once. Dual-operator machines cost more, but in a busy shop the extra output pays the difference back quickly.

How much tonnage does a hole take?

For A36 mild steel (about 65,000 psi tensile strength), the force to punch a hole in US tons is:

  • Round hole: punch diameter × material thickness × 80. A 3/8″ hole in 1/4″ plate takes .375 × .25 × 80 = 7.5 tons.
  • Shaped hole: one third of the punch perimeter × material thickness × 80. A 3/8 × 1″ slot (perimeter 2.75″) in 1/4″ plate takes (.33 × 2.75) × .25 × 80 = 18.2 tons.

For other materials, multiply the mild-steel figure by a material factor: aluminum ×0.38, half-hard copper ×0.52, quarter-hard brass ×0.70, cold-rolled steel ×1.24, 0.50 % carbon steel ×1.50 — and about ×1.5 for stainless steel.

The stations, one by one

Punching

The punch station punches, stamps or embosses; its removable table lets you punch channel and angle with the flange or leg down.

Flat bar and plate shearing

The bar and plate shear makes straight or angled cuts in mild steel flat bar and plate, burr-free and with little distortion, up to the capacity in the machine's specifications. A hold-down set by hand crank keeps the material flat during the cut.

Angle shearing and mitring

The angle shear cuts mild steel angle square or at a mitre, with clean, burr-free edges; an oversized hold-down set by a thumb crank holds the angle in place.

Round and square bar

The bar shear — or multi-shear tooling with bump dies — cuts round and square mild steel bar without distortion or burrs.

Notching

The notching station makes a three-sided cut in bar, plate or angle — rectangular, angled or shaped — burr-free and with little distortion.

V-notching (optional)

The V-notch tool makes a two-sided 90° cut in flat bar, plate or angle — the cut you need to fold angle iron into a frame.

Pipe notching (optional)

The pipe-notch tool cuts a saddle in the end of a mild steel pipe so it fits against another pipe for a tee joint.

Press brake tooling (optional)

Brake tooling bends flat bar, bar and angle in steps up to 90°, which turns the ironworker into a small press brake for brackets and clips.

Electric stroke control

Stroke control lets the operator shorten the up and down stroke with two adjustments. Use it to control bends with the brake tooling, to set the depth for embossing and bump dies, or simply to make more strokes per minute on the punch, notch and shear stations.

Lubrication

Grease the machine at least once every 8 hours of work — it is the single most important thing you can do to make an ironworker last. A central lubrication pump feeds every point at once.
