Plasma, laser or oxy-fuel?

A CNC plasma table cuts any electrically conductive metal — mild steel, stainless, aluminum, galvanized and coated plate — with a jet of ionised gas, from a DXF drawing, at a fraction of the price of a laser. Its sweet spot is plate from about 1/8″ to 1″: brackets, gussets, base plates, flanges, trailer and agricultural parts, signs and metal art.

  • Edge. A plasma cut is wider than a laser cut, with a slight bevel and some dross to knock off. That is fine for parts that will be welded, painted or galvanized.
  • Holes. Holes smaller than about one and a half times the plate thickness come out tapered. Drill or ream bolt holes that must fit closely.
  • Thin sheet and fine detail. A fiber laser cuts thin and medium sheet faster, with a narrow, square edge and small, precise holes.
  • Thick plate. Above about 1″ to 1-1/2″, oxy-fuel cutting becomes the economical process.

Many fabricators start with plasma for plate and add a laser when the sheet-metal work grows.

Which plasma source?

The source sets the thickness, not the table. As a guide for clean production cuts in mild steel on the tables on this page:

  • 45 A: about 5/16″
  • 65 A: about 3/8″
  • 85 A: about 1/2″
  • 100 A: severs about 1″

Hypertherm and other sources are available on request. Two details matter when you choose. Piercing (starting a cut in the middle of the plate) handles less thickness than starting from the edge, so size the source by the thickest plate you pierce. Consumables (electrode, nozzle, shield and swirl ring) wear with every start, so their life and price set much of the running cost. Ask for both before you choose a source.

Air plasma needs clean, dry compressed air. Water and oil in the air shorten consumable life and spoil the cut. Fit a dryer and filters, and in a Canadian winter drain the condensate from the lines, because cold air lines collect water.

The table: drives, height control and fume collection

  • Drives. Servo motors with rack-and-pinion or precision guides hold accuracy at speed and on heavy plate. Stepper motors are fine on compact tables for lighter work.
  • Torch height control. Automatic height control, which reads the arc voltage, keeps the torch at the right height as the plate warps from the heat. Without it, cut quality and consumable life suffer.
  • Frame. The frame must carry your thickest plate (the tables here carry 1″) without the slats sagging. Slats are consumables and are replaced as they are cut away.
  • Fume collection. A water table catches sparks and most of the fumes in water under the plate, and is simple and quiet. A downdraft table pulls the fumes down into a dust collector and keeps the plate dry. Cutting galvanized or coated plate makes fumes that must be extracted, whichever table you choose.

Software, files and pipe cutting

Parts are drawn in CAD, nested on the plate by the CAM software with the right lead-ins, kerf and cut order, and sent to the table. The compact tables run their programs from a USB stick with no computer. Good nesting saves more plate than any other setting, so try the software with your own parts before you buy.

For pipe, a CNC plasma pipe cutter turns the pipe in chucks while the torch cuts copes, saddles, holes and cut-offs, for structural and process piping from Ø2″ to 11-3/4″ with walls to 1/2″. It is the economical alternative to a tube laser when the pipe is large and the tolerance is that of a weld joint.

Safety and installation

The plasma arc gives off strong ultraviolet light. Operators and passers-by need shaded eye protection or a screened area around the table, and every table needs fume extraction. Ground the table properly: plasma is electrically noisy, and a good earth ground keeps the CNC and the height control from being disturbed.
