Which machines does a woodworking shop need?

Rough lumber is milled in a fixed order: the jointer flattens one face and squares an edge to it, the thickness planer makes the other face parallel and brings the board to thickness, and the table saw rips it to width and crosscuts it to length. The band saw cuts curves and resaws thick boards into thinner ones. Joinery machines — a mortiser or a slot boring machine — cut the mortises and dowel holes, and a sander finishes the parts. A dust collector serves all of them. A shop with room for one machine can start with a jointer-planer or a multifunction machine that planes, saws, mortises and drills on one motor.

Choosing the jointer and the thickness planer

  • Width. The jointer's width is the widest board you can flatten, so it limits your stock: 6″ for small work, 8″ for most furniture. The planer takes the full width of the board, 15″ or 20″.
  • Table length. Long jointer tables (55-1/2″ and 76″) support long boards from start to finish, so they come out straight instead of following their own bow.
  • Thickness range. The planers take stock up to 8″ thick and down to 3/16″; the jointer-planers thickness up to 6.3 – 9.1″.
  • Jointer-planer. One cutterblock does both jobs in the floor space of one machine. The tables lift for thicknessing, so you change over between the two; separate machines are faster when both run all day.

Spiral carbide cutterhead or straight knives?

A spiral (helical) cutterhead has many small square carbide inserts set in a spiral around the head. Each insert takes a short shearing cut, so the machine is quieter, figured and interlocked grain tears out less and the surface needs less sanding. When an insert is chipped it is turned to a fresh edge in a minute, instead of a full set of knives being reground and reset. Straight knives cost less and are fine for softwood and general work. The WJ jointers and WJP planers have spiral carbide heads; the WJ10X40 and the multifunction machines have straight knives.

Table saw or band saw?

The table saw is the machine for straight cuts: ripping solid wood, cutting sheet goods to size, crosscutting with a miter gauge or sled, and dadoes with a dado stack. Size it by the rip capacity — 30″ to the right of the blade for solid wood, 52″ to cut a 4 × 8 sheet to any width — and use the riving knife, the guard and push sticks every time. The band saw cuts curves and resaws: its resaw height (12 – 13″) is the widest board it can slice into thinner boards, and its throat is the widest piece it can pass between the blade and the frame.

Mortisers and boring machines

A hollow-chisel mortiser cuts a square-cornered mortise: a bit turns inside a square chisel that is driven into the wood, so the tenon needs no rounding. A slot boring machine (slot mortiser) mills a slot with round ends by moving the bit across the work, and also bores dowel holes; it is faster for production and makes slots for loose tenons. A horizontal boring machine drills the dowel holes in the edges and ends of panels and frames.

Dust collection

Planers, jointers and table saws make chips faster than a shop vacuum can take them, and fine dust is a fire and health risk. The planer makes the most chips and needs the most air. A single-stage collector such as the DC900 (940 CFM, two 4″ inlets, a 30-micron filter bag) serves one or two machines at a time: keep the hose runs short, close the inlets of the machines that are not running, and empty the bag before it is full.

Questions we get asked about woodworking machinery

Jointer, thickness planer or jointer-planer — what do I need?

A jointer flattens one face of a rough board and squares an edge to it; a thickness planer then makes the other face parallel and brings the board to thickness. You need both to mill rough lumber. A jointer-planer (TDM) does both jobs with one cutterblock in the space of one machine — the tables lift for thicknessing — at the cost of a changeover between the two; separate machines are faster when both run all day.

Spiral carbide cutterhead or straight knives?

A spiral (helical) cutterhead has many small carbide inserts set in a spiral: it cuts with a shearing action, so it is quieter, leaves a cleaner surface on figured and interlocked grain and tears out less, and a chipped insert is turned to a fresh edge in a minute instead of a set of knives reground and reset. Straight knives cost less to buy and are fine for softwood and general work. The WJ jointers and WJP planers have spiral carbide heads; the WJ10X40 and the multifunction machines have straight knives.

Which table saw — 30″ or 52″ rip, hand wheels or touch screen?

The rip capacity is the widest cut to the right of the blade: 30″ is enough for solid wood and for cutting a sheet of plywood to 30″ parts; 52″ cuts a 4 × 8 sheet to any width, which a cabinet shop needs. All WTS10 saws take a 10″ blade with 3-3/8″ of cut at 90°; on the S models the blade height and angle are typed on a touch screen and shown digitally — faster and repeatable set-ups for production.

Do I need a dust collector?

Yes for every planer, jointer and table saw: they make chips faster than a shop vacuum can take them, and fine dust is a health risk. A single-stage collector like the DC900 — 940 CFM, two 4″ inlets — serves one or two machines at a time; keep the hose runs short and connect the machine that is running.

What voltage do these machines run on?

Three-phase machines are supplied for 600 V as standard with a CSA-approved panel; 208 V, 240 V and 480 V 3-phase are available on request, and 240 V single-phase on models whose main motor is 2.2 kW (3 HP) or less — a machine with a motor above 2.2 kW must run on three-phase power and cannot be converted to 240 V single-phase. Manual machines have no electrics.
